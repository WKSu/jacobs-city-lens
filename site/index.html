The Death and Life of Great American Cities, 1961

Jacobs City Lens

Jane Jacobs named four conditions a city district needs to generate diversity: mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of different ages, and enough people. This site measures them with open data, hexagon by hexagon, and compares the old districts she would have recognised with the planned ones she argued against.

The four generators, and what surrounds them

Old districts and planned ones

Each dot is a district's median hexagon, placed by where it falls among all hexagons in the city. Scales are flipped where needed, so further right is always closer to what Jacobs asked for: shorter blocks, more mix, more people.

How dense is dense enough?

Border vacuums: activity thins out near large single uses

Jacobs saw that rail yards, expressways, campuses and big parks form "dead ends" for the streets around them (ch. 14). Each panel counts activity on ordinary land in 50 m bands from the edge of one kind of vacuum. The dashed line is the city-wide level; a line that climbs from left to right is a border vacuum at work.

Parks and the uses around them

"Generalized neighborhood parks" thrive only when the district around them brings people for different reasons at different times (ch. 5). Each dot is a park; parks inside a compared district take its colour. Size alone says little: look at what surrounds it.
