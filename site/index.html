After The Death and Life of Great American Cities, 1961

Jacobs City Lens

Jane Jacobs named four conditions a city district needs to generate diversity: mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of different ages, and enough people. This site is a way to learn how she looked at a city: each idea, the scene she saw it in, how it works, and an honest test of what the city's open data can and can't show about it.

Before the four conditions

Why cities are worth it

Before Jacobs said what a district needs, she said what a city is for. The first part of her book is about “the peculiar nature of cities”: what a big city does that a town or a suburb can't. The planners of her day saw the crowded old city as a problem to clear away. She saw something that worked, often very well, and set out to explain why.

Everything else follows from that. A town works because people know each other. A city has to work for people who don't. Jacobs found five things a lively city gives the strangers who live in it.

  1. Strangers can feel safe

    Safety on a city street doesn't come mainly from the police. It comes from people: shopkeepers, neighbours at the window, passers-by, all keeping half an eye on the street without being asked. On Hudson Street, where she lived, Jacobs called the daily rhythm of these people coming and going a “sidewalk ballet”.

    A well-used city street is apt to be a safe street. A deserted city street is apt to be unsafe. Chapter 2

  2. You can be private and still belong

    In a city you can chat with the grocer every morning without ever inviting them home. Jacobs thought this was a strength, not a weakness. Many small, casual contacts on the pavement build trust between people, while everyone keeps their private life to themselves.

    The trust of a city street is formed over time from many, many little public sidewalk contacts. Chapter 3

  3. Children grow up among adults

    Planners wanted children off the street and into playgrounds. Jacobs pointed out that on a busy pavement, children play in sight of adults who aren't their parents but who will step in when needed. That is how children learn that strangers look out for each other.

    People must take a modicum of public responsibility for each other even if they have no ties to each other. Chapter 4

  4. Small and unusual things can survive

    A shop selling only maps, a theatre for one kind of music, a two-person workshop: none of these could live on the customers of a small town. A big city has so many people close together that even a rare taste adds up to enough customers. That is why new firms and new ideas so often start in cities.

    Cities are natural generators of diversity and prolific incubators of new enterprises and ideas of all kinds. Chapter 7

  5. The city is made by everybody

    A city is not designed once, from above. It is built and rebuilt by millions of people making their own plans, trying things, failing and trying again. Jacobs saw it as a huge experiment that planners should learn from instead of replacing.

    Cities have the capability of providing something for everybody, only because, and only when, they are created by everybody. The Death and Life of Great American Cities

None of this happens by itself. Every one of these gifts depends on people being out on the street, at many hours of the day, for many different reasons. Jacobs' big question was what makes that happen, and her answer was in how a district is built. The data on this site can't see safety, trust or children at play. It can see the buildings and streets she said produce them, and that is where the rest of the site begins.

How Jacobs saw a city

Jacobs started from the street: who is on it, when, and why. She argued that four physical conditions bring people out at all hours, that those people keep shops and small firms going, and that busy streets are safer and better loved. Data can see the buildings and streets well. It can't see people, so the middle of the chain is described in words, and each tab says plainly how far the data gets.

Six districts: does the idea fit real places?

The tabs explain each condition on its own. Here they come together in six Rotterdam districts: two old ones that grew street by street, and four that were planned in one go. If Jacobs was right, the districts that meet more of her conditions should have more going on in their streets.

1Which conditions does each district meet?

Each square is one hexagon of about 10 ha inside the district. It is filled when that hexagon is in the city's top 40% for the condition, the same test as the "All four together" tab. The bar on the right counts shops, cafés and other places per hectare: a visible trace of street life, not a count of people.

2What do the conditions look like from above?

The same 800 × 800 m of each district, at the same scale, in the same order as above. Buildings are black, streets are grey lines. You can see three of the four conditions before any statistics:

  • Short blocks: many streets crossing, small blocks between them.
  • Concentration: buildings packed along the streets, not standing in open space.
  • Aged buildings: many small, irregular buildings next to big new ones, a sign they were built at different times.

Mixed uses can't be seen from above. The squares under each plan repeat which conditions the district meets.

Every measure, district by district

Each dot is a district's median hexagon, placed by where it falls among all hexagons in the city. Scales are flipped where needed, so further right is always closer to what Jacobs asked for: shorter blocks, more mix, more people.

Border vacuums: activity thins out near large single uses

A closer look at

Jacobs saw that rail yards, expressways, campuses and big parks form "dead ends" for the streets around them (ch. 14). Each panel counts activity on ordinary land in 50 m bands from the edge of one kind of vacuum. The dashed line is the city-wide level. A line that climbs from left to right in places per hectare looks like a border vacuum, but switch to places per 100 addresses: near most edges it stays flat, so the dip comes mostly from fewer homes near the edge, not from people avoiding the shops there.

Parks and the uses around them

A closer look at

"Generalized neighborhood parks" thrive only when the district around them brings people for different reasons at different times (ch. 5). Each dot is a park; parks inside a compared district take its colour. Size alone says little: look at what surrounds it.

When is anything open?

A closer look at

Jacobs' first generator asks for uses that bring people out "on different schedules" (ch. 8). A district of offices is busy for eight hours and empty for the other sixteen, and no count of shops can tell it apart from a district of cafés and homes. Each grid is one week: days down, hours across, coloured by the share of that district's places that are open at that hour.

Read it as the shape of a week, not as how busy a district is. Only about a tenth of places publish opening hours in OpenStreetMap, and not evenly: a shopping centre full of chains is tagged far better than a street of independent shops, which is why the post-war estates here look the most uniformly open. Each panel says how many of its district's known places it is actually drawn from. A district whose colour changes through the day has uses on different schedules; one that switches on and off together does not.
